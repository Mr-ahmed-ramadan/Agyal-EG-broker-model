Field

Innovative idea or technology

Agyal is a white-label, FIX-native platform that lets an FRA-licensed brokerage firm offer retail investors digital access to Egyptian government treasury bills and bonds, under the broker's own brand. The broker stays the licensed party and holds all client money; Agyal provides the software only and never holds client money or securities.

The model is established internationally but has not been applied to Egyptian retail fixed income in this form. Four things are new here. First, small ticket sizes become economic, because the cost to serve is software rather than branch staff — today most retail savers reach treasury bills through a bank branch, with high minimums and paperwork. Second, every retail order is competitively priced: the platform sends a FIX 4.4 quote request to all connected partner banks at once and shows the client the best price, which retail-size fixed-income orders do not normally receive. Third, onboarding is fully digital, with no branch visit: identity verification, automated AML and PEP screening, risk profiling and MCDR unified-code registration all happen in the application. Fourth, pricing is itemised: the client sees their net yield and each deduction separately rather than an undisclosed spread.

Because one platform can stand behind many licensed brokers, the innovation reaches the market through existing licensed firms rather than requiring a new licensed entrant.

On readiness: the platform is functionally complete end to end and covered by an automated test suite that exercises onboarding and screening, custody linkage, deposits, buying and selling over FIX, settlement, coupons and maturity with tax withholding, withdrawals with maker-checker approval, and a double-entry ledger that nets to zero. Several external integrations run against simulators: partner-bank FIX, eKYC, AML screening, MCDR confirmations and SMS. Each sits behind a defined provider interface with a working implementation on the other side, so connecting a live licensed provider is an integration rather than a redesign, and doing so is part of what we would do under the Authority's guidance. Separately, the capture and retention of identity evidence is not yet built, by choice: the evidential standard is one of the questions we put to the Authority, and the answer determines what must be captured and kept.

If space is tight: A white-label platform letting FRA-licensed brokers offer retail investors digital access to government treasury bills and bonds under their own brand. Four innovations for this market: economic small-ticket access; competitive multi-bank pricing on every retail order via FIX; fully digital onboarding with eKYC, AML screening and MCDR registration; and itemised disclosure of net yield and every deduction. The broker remains the licensed party and holds all client money — Agyal provides software only. The platform is built and tested end to end; several external integrations currently run against simulators and would be connected to live licensed providers under the Authority's guidance, and identity-evidence capture awaits the Authority's evidential standard before it is built.

Benefit to customers and the market

For customers. Lower minimums open sovereign paper to savers currently priced out of it. Remote onboarding reaches governorates with thin branch coverage, and savers who cannot give up a working day to visit a branch. Clients see their net yield and every deduction before committing, with a projection of expected proceeds at maturity and tax shown separately. The experience is built in Arabic for first-time investors, with risk disclosure in plain language. Every client is onboarded with full KYC and every security is registered to the client's own unified code at MCDR — not pooled in a nominee structure.

For the market. Smaller brokerage firms gain a retail fixed-income capability they could not economically build alone, which strengthens firms currently under competitive pressure. Domestic retail participation in government funding broadens, consistent with national objectives on savings mobilisation and financial inclusion. Savings held informally move into regulated, registered, KYC'd holdings. Competitive quoting for retail-size orders puts gentle downward pressure on retail spreads. And a traditionally manual segment becomes digital, with a complete electronic audit trail available to the broker's compliance function and to the Authority.

We propose to measure this rather than assert it: share of participants new to government securities, median ticket size, onboarding completion rate, time to first trade, and a comprehension check on whether clients can correctly state their net yield and deductions.

If space is tight: Customers gain access to government paper at far lower minimums, without a branch visit, with net yield and every deduction disclosed up front, in Arabic and designed for first-time investors — each holding registered under the client's own MCDR code. The market gains retail fixed-income capability for smaller licensed brokers that could not build it alone, broader domestic participation in government funding, formalisation of informal savings, competitive pressure on retail spreads, and a full electronic audit trail in a traditionally manual segment. We propose measuring impact directly: share of participants new to government securities, median ticket size, onboarding completion, and a client comprehension check on yield and deductions.

Roadmap — vision, market introduction, regulatory alignment, management, global experience

Vision and market introduction. Launch is staged, each stage gated on evidence rather than time. Stage 1, readiness: connect live eKYC and AML providers, complete FIX certification with [2–3] partner banks, finalise the client agreement and risk disclosure — no client money. Stage 2, controlled pilot: [20] clients with real money at the lowest limits, every order additionally reviewed by the broker's dealer, daily reconciliation. Stage 3, open cohort: up to [100] clients recruited publicly within caps. Stage 4, scale: on the Authority's confirmation of permanent status, caps lift on an agreed schedule and the platform is offered to further licensed brokers. Proposed limits during guided phases, adjustable to whatever the Authority prefers: per-client holding [EGP 250,000], per-order [EGP 100,000], aggregate exposure [EGP 10,000,000], duration [9 months]. Scope is restricted to Egyptian government bills and bonds in EGP for resident retail individuals 18+; no corporate paper, equities, derivatives, FX, margin, leverage, short selling, discretionary management or advice.

Regulatory alignment and legal framework. The licensed broker — not Agyal — is the regulated party in every client relationship and every trade. The broker owns the client relationship, the KYC/AML decision and suitability; holds client money in its own segregated account; and owns the books and records. Execution is by a partner bank on the broker's instruction. Custody and the unified code sit with the broker or custodian via MCDR. Agyal provides software, hosting and data processing. Agyal never holds client money or securities, and this is enforced in the software rather than by policy alone — every money-moving function is restricted to the broker's own finance and operations roles, and the platform-operator role has no access to any of them. Client cash exists only in the broker's segregated bank account, mirrored in a double-entry ledger whose trial balance must net to zero. Agyal is paid by the broker under a software agreement, never by the end client and never out of client money. Our working assumption, on which we seek confirmation, is that Agyal is properly characterised as an outsourced technology provider to licensed entities rather than an entity requiring its own licence. Data processing is as processor on the broker's instruction under Law 151/2020.

Management. [Founders and key staff, their capital-markets and technology experience, organisation chart, who holds compliance responsibility, shareholding and ultimate beneficial owners, funding position and runway.] Operationally, responsibilities are documented in an operations SOP covering the full client lifecycle — who performs each step, what is checked, what needs a second approver. Money movements use maker-checker approval. Every data change and sign-in is written to an immutable audit log available to the broker's compliance function and to the Authority on request. Service levels, audit rights, continuity, data return and exit terms sit in the technology agreement with each broker.

Global experience. Retail access to government securities through supervised digital channels is well established. The closest analogue is Brazil's Tesouro Direto, run by the National Treasury with B3, which distributes federal government bonds to retail investors through licensed brokers and agents — structurally the same division of roles proposed here. Also relevant: RBI Retail Direct in India, which opened direct retail access to government securities; TreasuryDirect in the United States; and DhowCSD in Kenya, which digitised retail access to government paper. On the provider side, platforms supplying onboarding, order routing and books-and-records software to licensed brokers operate in several regulated markets under outsourcing rules with defined audit, continuity and exit obligations. We would gladly prepare a fuller comparative note if that would assist.

Field

Specific areas where assistance is required

The platform is built; our open questions are regulatory rather than technical. In order of importance:

1. The correct regulatory status for a technology provider in this model. Is a provider that supplies onboarding workflow, order routing and books-and-records software to FRA-licensed brokers — while never holding client money or securities, never contracting with end clients and never exercising discretion — correctly treated as an outsourced service provider rather than an entity requiring its own licence? If registration under the Authority's outsourcing framework is the right destination, we would welcome guidance on criteria, process and the conditions that would attach. This is the question that most affects how we build and raise.

2. Evidential standards for fully remote onboarding. What evidence does the Authority expect for opening a retail securities relationship without a branch visit — acceptable documents, liveness and face-match thresholds, screening scope, record retention — and is remote onboarding sufficient for MCDR unified-code issuance?

3. Evidencing best execution for OTC fixed income. How should a broker evidence best execution where retail-size orders are priced by competing quotes from several banks, what records should be retained for each decision, and how should exceptions be documented?

4. Disclosure of yield-based pricing. Does itemised disclosure of the deduction from gross yield — shown pre-trade and on the contract note, each component separately identified — meet the Authority's transparency expectations for retail clients, and is any prescribed form or wording required?

5. Structure of the engagement and the partner requirement. Because the licensed broker is the regulated party, any live phase involving client money runs under the broker's licence. Should this application be made by Agyal, by the broker, or jointly — and may guidance be given before a licensed partner is signed, with a live phase conditional on naming one?

6. Proportionate limits and reporting. Are the proposed caps on cohort size, per-client holding and aggregate exposure in the range the Authority would consider appropriate, and what reporting would it wish to receive during a guided phase?

As the project develops we anticipate needing further guidance on client-money reconciliation and reporting expectations, complaint-handling standards for a digital-only channel, and the conditions for extending the platform to additional licensed brokers.

How the solution addresses the non-bank financial sector

The regulated activity the platform enables is securities brokerage, which sits within the Authority's perimeter: the client relationship, the securities account, suitability and custody are all those of an FRA-licensed brokerage firm, and securities are registered at MCDR under the Authority's supervision.

Partner banks appear in the model only as liquidity counterparties in an over-the-counter market — the role a primary dealer plays when a broker sources paper. No banking service is offered to the client through the platform: no deposit-taking, no payment service, no lending, no stored value. The client's money sits in the licensed broker's own segregated client account, an existing banking relationship of the broker's rather than a service the platform provides.

Agyal's own position is likewise a non-banking question — whether a technology provider to FRA-licensed brokers falls under the Authority's outsourcing framework — and that is the central matter on which guidance is sought.
